# Part Three: Day 22 and After

# Chapter 5: Keep Your Margin

Day 22 is the most dangerous day of the whole plan.

The novelty is gone. Nobody is congratulating you. There's no new chapter waiting tomorrow morning. And the habits you built are still new enough that skipping one feels harmless.

This is exactly where most programs die. Not in a dramatic crash. They just fade.

So for the next few weeks, your only job is to **keep your margin**. Don't add anything new. Don't start a big push. Just keep doing what's on your one page.

## Boring is the goal

The first 21 days had some energy to them. Every day was a little different. From here on, it should get a little boring. That's a good sign. Boring means automatic. Automatic means it's becoming your baseline.

If you notice yourself itching to do something bigger, write it down and save it for your first sprint in Chapter 6. For now, protect what you've built.

## The weekly check

Once a week, pick a time you'll remember, say Sunday evening or Friday lunch, and take five minutes to:

1. **Check your Peace of Mind balance.** Did the automatic transfer go through? Is it growing?
2. **Look at your tracker.** How many days this week did you do your one simple action? Your breath? Don't grade yourself. Just notice.
3. **Read your one page.** Does anything need to change? A habit that's too big? A cue that isn't working?
4. **Pick one thing to protect next week.** The habit most likely to slip. Give it a little extra attention.

That's it. Five minutes, once a week.

## Growing the Peace of Mind Account

Once you hit your first $500, keep the automatic transfer running and let the account grow in stages:

- **First:** $500. The first win.
- **Next:** $1,000.
- **Then:** your Peace of Mind number, about one month of essentials.
- **After that:** keep building toward three months of essentials, at whatever pace your life allows.

Each stage changes more of life's surprises from emergencies into expenses. And somewhere along the way, if the storms are kind, the account starts to become that opportunity fund: money that lets you say yes when something good comes along.

## Adding the next thing

After about a month of keeping your margin, when your habits feel automatic, you can add **one** new small action. Choose it using the same rules:

- Small enough to survive your worst week.
- Specific enough that someone could tell whether you did it.
- Hooked onto a routine you already have.

One at a time. Master one, then add another. Depth beats breadth. The biggest threat to a good baseline isn't laziness. It's getting excited and piling on so many new habits that the whole thing collapses under its own weight.

## If the app helps

The free companion app has a "Keep Your Margin" mode for exactly this stretch. It keeps a simple daily check-in going after Day 21, so there's still a small nudge each day even when the challenge is over. You'll find it at **bendyourbaseline.com/guide**.

## Bringing the people you live with along

The people closest to you will notice the changes before anyone else. Sometimes they'll cheer. Sometimes they'll push back, usually without meaning to. The cookies still show up. The spontaneous dinner out still sounds fun. The "you're no fun anymore" comment still stings.

A few things tend to help:

- **Explain your why, once, calmly.** "I want a cushion so the next car repair doesn't wreck our month." "I want to have more energy after work." People support a reason more easily than a rule.
- **Start with changes that don't affect anyone else.** Your breathing. Your lunchtime walk. Your automatic transfer. Nobody has to give anything up.
- **Invite, don't recruit.** "I'm walking after dinner, want to come?" works better than "we should all be walking after dinner."
- **Don't preach.** The fastest way to lose support is to make someone feel judged for their own baseline.
- **Share the wins.** When the Peace of Mind Account turns a surprise bill into a shrug, say so. That's the moment people start asking how you did it.

If someone in your household wants to join you, great. Start them at Day 0. Everyone works on their own numbers. You just get to cheer each other on.

# Chapter 6: Your First Sprint

Once your baseline is bending and your habits feel automatic, usually a month or two after Day 21, you might be ready to push.

Remember the runners from Chapter 4. Hard seasons, easy seasons, a pumpkin run in between. The goal is the same here: a focused burst of higher intensity, with recovery and rest built in, sitting on top of a baseline that holds when the burst ends.

## Before you start: the one-question test

Ask yourself: *if this sprint ended tomorrow and I did nothing else, would my baseline hold?*

If the answer is no, you're not ready. Go back to keeping your margin for a few more weeks. A sprint without a bent baseline underneath it is just another trip on the yo-yo.

## Building the sprint

Pick **one** pillar. Just one. Then work through the four ingredients.

**1. Specificity.** Write down three things:

- **Where you are now.** Your actual number. "I have $900 in Peace of Mind." "I walk 5,500 steps a day." "I rate my stress a 7 most afternoons."
- **Where you want to be.** "I want $2,500." "I want to run a 5K without stopping." "I want most afternoons to be a 4."
- **When.** 60 days? 90 days?

The size of the gap and the length of time decide how much intensity you need. If the gap is big and the time is short, the plan will be hard. Be honest about whether you can live with that for the whole sprint. If you can't, give yourself more time or shrink the goal.

**2. Recovery.** Plan your easy days before you start. For a fitness sprint, that might be three hard days and four easy ones each week. For a money sprint, it might mean one week each month where you don't push extra savings, you just let the automatic transfer run. For a stress sprint, it might be one evening a week with no plans at all.

The easy days aren't a break from the plan. They *are* the plan. They're what make the hard days possible.

**3. Rest.** Decide now how the sprint ends. Put the end date on your calendar. Then plan a lighter stretch afterward, maybe two weeks, before you even think about the next push. Something fun. Your version of the pumpkin run.

**4. The baseline underneath.** Keep your one page going the whole time. The sprint sits on top of your habits, not in place of them.

## During the sprint

Knowing the end date helps. When a hard week comes, you can tell yourself: *I've got three more weeks of this, then I get two easier ones.* That's much easier to live with than "forever."

If you get hurt, sick, or overwhelmed partway through, stop the sprint. Don't stop the baseline. Drop back to your one page, recover, and start a new sprint later. Remember: the three freshmen who wouldn't take an easy day all missed the season.

## After the sprint

When the sprint ends, you will lose some of what you gained. That's normal. Nobody keeps the peak.

But look at where you land. If your baseline held, you'll settle somewhere higher than where you started the sprint. You lose it slower, because the floor went up.

Then rest. Then, when you're ready, pick the next pillar.

# Chapter 7: When the Storm Hits

Sooner or later, a real storm will come. A job loss. A medical bill. A family crisis. A season where everything falls apart at once.

This chapter is for that season.

## First: this is what the margin was for

If you have to spend your Peace of Mind money, spend it. That's its job. The account didn't fail. It worked. The storm became an expense instead of an emergency, or at least a smaller emergency than it would have been.

Don't feel guilty about using it. Feel glad you had it.

## Shrink everything to the smallest version

When life gets chaotic, don't try to keep your whole one page going. Drop every habit to its hard day version:

- **Money:** Keep the automatic transfer running if you can, even if you lower it to $5. If you can't, pause it, and write down the date you'll restart it.
- **Body:** One small thing a day. A walk around the block. Water before meals. Whatever is easiest.
- **Stress:** The breath. Four rounds, once a day. This is the one to protect hardest, because this is when you need it most.

The goal during a storm isn't progress. It's keeping the thread. A tiny habit that survives the storm is worth more than a big one you drop and have to rebuild from scratch.

## Use your Reset Protocol

This is what you wrote it for on Day 19. Pull it out. Read it. Do the smallest restart. Ask what got in the way. Adjust the system, not your opinion of yourself.

## Refill, then rebuild

When the storm passes, rebuild in order:

1. **Refill the Peace of Mind Account** first. Bump your automatic transfer for a while if you can, even by $10 a week, until you're back where you were.
2. **Restore your one page**, one habit at a time. Start with the one that was easiest to keep.
3. **Only then** think about new habits or a sprint.

## You haven't gone back to the start

Here's the thing to remember when you're in the middle of it. Even if you slip a long way, you're not starting over. You know your numbers. You have your one page. You know what works for you and what doesn't. You know how to restart small.

The yo-yo sends you back to the beginning. A bent baseline doesn't. The storm may knock you down, but you come back to a higher floor than the one you had before you started.

# Chapter 8: The Organizational Yo-Yo

This chapter is for anyone who leads other people: a manager, a business owner, an HR leader, a committee chair, a coach. If that's not you, feel free to skip it, or pass it along to someone it fits.

Everything in this book so far has been about you. Here's the part most people don't see coming: **organizations live on the yo-yo too.**

## The new direction, every quarter

I mentioned it back in Chapter 1. In many workplaces, every quarter brings a new initiative. Each one is introduced as *the new direction*, and a quarter or two later, it's replaced by the next one, introduced with the same words.

The wellness program. The engagement survey action plan. The safety campaign. The new software. The AI pilot. Launch energy, a strong start, a slow fade, and a quiet return to the way things were.

The people launching them mean it every time. That's what makes it a design problem, not a character problem. An initiative is a push. Pushes end. And what's waiting when the push ends is the organization's baseline: what everyone does by default when nobody is driving.

## Four things programs lean on

Many workplace programs rely on one or more of four things. Each can be useful. Each tends to fail when the program rests on it alone.

**1. More information: "we'll train them."** The webinar. The workbook. The lunch-and-learn. The idea is that people struggle because they don't know enough. But a stressed, busy person usually doesn't need more information. They already know they *should*. Knowing was never the problem.

**2. Motivation: "we'll inspire them."** The poster. The challenge. The stretch goal. But telling someone to save 15 percent when they can't cover a surprise car repair doesn't motivate them. It can make them feel worse, and feeling worse leads to avoiding the whole subject.

**3. A tool expected to carry adoption on its own: "we'll buy a platform."** Lots of features, lots of fields, badges and points. But every step (download it, log in, enter your data, remember to come back) is a place to lose someone before the habit forms.

**4. One person championing it: "we'll assign a champion."** A coach, a counselor, a designated expert. That can help. But it often depends on the struggling person raising their hand, and one person only has so much time.

Look at the four together:

> **Each one tends to wait for the struggling person to come to it.**
>
> **And each one tends to lead with information instead of a win.**

## What tends to hold

The organizational version of bending the baseline looks a lot like the personal version:

- **One action, small enough that people keep doing it in a bad week.** If it can't survive a chaotic month, it's a phase, not a habit.
- **Delivered to people, not waiting for them to show up.** Put it where they already are.
- **A win before any teaching.** Let people do one small thing and feel it work before you ask them to learn anything. A win earns the right to teach.
- **Something they can always see their place on.** A path, not a pile of options.

> **A program is a push with an end date. A path is something you can always see your place on.**

## A two-minute check for any initiative

You can run this on anything your organization has in motion right now:

1. **Which of the four is it leaning on?** Information, motivation, a tool, or one champion? (Some lean on three at once.)
2. **Does it wait for people to come to it?**
3. **Does anyone get a win before they're asked to learn something?**
4. **Could someone keep doing it during their worst week of the year?**
5. **What happens when the person driving it moves on?**

If the answers make you wince, that's useful. It means you can fix the design before month three instead of after.

# A Final Word

The storms of life will keep coming. They always do.

But you don't have to meet every one of them at the same old floor. Every small habit you keep, every $5 you move into your Peace of Mind Account, every round of breathing you take before a hard meeting, bends your baseline a little.

It won't look like much from one day to the next. It isn't supposed to. Look back in a year, though, and you may barely recognize the floor you started from.

You can't change your destination overnight. But you can change your direction today.

*Go bend your baseline.*

## The 4x4x4x4 Breath

3. Hold for 4.

5. Hold empty for 4.
6. Repeat at least four times.

# A Note on Sources

I kept numbers in this book to a minimum on purpose. The two I used:

- **The $400 emergency expense.** Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025* (May 2026). 63 percent of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense exclusively with cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement.
- **Missing a day while building a habit.** Phillippa Lally, Cornelia van Jaarsveld, Henry Potts, and Jane Wardle, "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world," *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40, no. 6 (2010): 998 to 1009. Participants chose a daily eating, drinking, or activity behavior and tracked it for 12 weeks; a single missed day had little effect on later habit formation. The study also found wide variation in how long habits took to feel automatic, which is why this book treats 21 days as a foundation rather than a finish line.

The Jim Rohn line in Chapter 3 is widely attributed to him. The idea of attaching a new habit to an existing routine (Day 18) has been popularized by several writers on habits, often under the name "habit stacking."

This book is for general education. It isn't personalized financial, medical, or mental health advice. If you have a medical condition, check with your doctor before changing your exercise or eating. If you're struggling with serious stress, anxiety, or depression, please talk with a qualified professional.
